## 1. Nobody Saw Her Leave

Elena **went missing** on a Tuesday, somewhere **on the way** between the university and her mother's house.

Two officers **introduced themselves** at the door and asked to **look through** her room. Her **fiancé**, Marek, agreed **reluctantly**, and only if he could **be present**.

He **glanced** at the desk before they reached it. The younger officer **noticed** the **hesitation** — small, but **noticeably** wrong.

The **circumstances** were strange. Elena's usual **route** home took forty minutes; that evening she had **refused** a lift from her **uncle** and decided to **hitchhike** instead, something she had never done. "**This suggests that** she was avoiding someone," the officer said.

On the second day the **authorities** released her photograph, and **law enforcement** searched the woods for three days. Marek **searched for** her online, phone always **at the ready**, telling everyone she **may still be alive**. A journalist pushed a microphone at him. "**What is it like**," she asked, "when somebody just vanishes?" He said he could not sleep, that losing **someone close to him** had broken something. He behaved **suspiciously** from the very beginning.

A neighbour finally **witnessed** something useful: two people **dragging** a heavy bag towards a car at midnight. That detail **is connected to** everything else.

When police reached the house, the couple **managed to escape** through the garden, though not far. Marek came **face to face with** the officer he had lied to for a week.

Elena was alive. They found her in the woman's garage, thin and frightened, and an ambulance **took her back home** before dawn.

Her mother, who had **started acting strangely** on the very first day, sat down on the kitchen floor and cried. "It **is over**," she said. She had never believed in **fate**, but that night she thanked it anyway.

## 2. The Mirror in the Fraternity House

The **fraternity** house had a mirror in the hallway that nobody liked.

It began as a game of **Truth or Dare**. A first-year student was told **to look into the mirror** and say a name three times. He did it, laughing. Nothing happened. Ten minutes later he **fell dead onto the floor**.

The **coroner** found a massive **cerebral hemorrhage** and no wound at all. "Whatever **caused** this," she wrote, "was not **violent** in any ordinary sense."

**The crime scene** was the hallway itself. **While examining the crime scene**, Detective Ruiz found something under the bathroom **sink**: **pieces of shed human skin**, thin as paper. Something had **shed** its skin in this house.

Daniel, as it turned out, **is responsible for** nothing. He had been in another city. But his **envy** was real, and something in the house had been listening. It was **carrying out his secret wish** without ever asking him.

The thing in the mirror was **vengeful**. She had been **murdered** in that house in 1961 and **buried** under the garden. She used the glass **to lure** students, and afterwards she **haunted** whoever had looked in.

Ruiz decided **to take on** the case herself. A **reverend** agreed **to perform** the old rite. They found her **hideout** — a crawl space behind the cellar wall — **dug up** the bones, and laid **a cross** on the ground beside them.

Daniel arrived at the last moment, drunk and **carrying weapons** he did not know how to use. One of the brothers **knocked** him **out** before he could **injure** anyone; he was **wounded** only where he hit the step.

They **carried out** the burning at dawn. There was no **brutality** in it, nothing done **intentionally** cruel — only fire and salt.

The mirror cracked from corner to corner, and the year ended, for once, **in the brothers' favor**.

## 3. Flight 402 and the Road After

Maya survived Flight 402, and she has never flown since.

The plane **took off** normally. **Within seconds** of reaching altitude something in the left engine failed. The report said the failure was **caused by** a **defect** in a single part, not by the pilots. Smoke came through the **air vents**. The captain fought **to level** the aircraft, but they **had almost no time left**.

She remembers the noise, then nothing — she **lost consciousness** somewhere above the trees. She woke inside the **wreckage**, **barely** able to move, the tail section **engulfed in** flames. A farmer **rescued** her. Of ninety-one passengers, eleven lived.

Now she drives everywhere.

Last spring she **hit the road** before sunrise and **arrived in** a small town in Arizona to photograph a new **housing development** built **on former Native American land**. The builders had cleared an old **camp site** at the edge of it. **For the first time** in years, Maya felt almost calm.

Then the small things started. **After a while** she noticed that the **townspeople** never **went outside** after dark, and that the children were **terrified of** the gardens. At the motel she found a **cockroach** in her bag and a note under the door that said only: *leave*. **That's really reassuring**, she thought.

On the third evening she **pulled over** beside a fresh **pit** at the edge of the site. In the headlights the ground seemed **to come to life**: **a huge swarm of insects** pouring out of the earth, **layer** upon layer of them. **Termites**, and something older underneath.

She **got scared of** the sound long before she understood the sight. Something **chased** her car for half a kilometre and then stopped, exactly at the fence.

In the show home **next to** the pit, the builders had opened a **cellar** that appeared on no plan. Inside it lay a tarnished silver **goblet** and a length of bare copper **wire**. A worker who touched that wire took an **electric shock** strong enough to stop his heart for nine seconds.

Maya photographed all of it. Surviving twice is not a **coincidence**, she thought; it is a warning. She **ended up** selling the pictures to a newspaper, and she never went back.

## 4. My Grandfather's Psalm

**At first** my grandfather refused to go to hospital at all.

He had broken a **kneecap** falling off a ladder at seventy-nine, which he considered a private embarrassment. When I visited, he said he had **nothing to complain** about. **However**, I could see the pain in how carefully he moved.

He asked me to bring his old book of psalms — a family **heritage**, held together with a leather **cord**. He had read it **the day before** he married my grandmother, he said, and in every hard week since.

I am not religious. **Nevertheless**, I sat and listened while he began **to chant** the second psalm quietly, the way he had learned it as a boy.

*Why do the nations **rage**, and the peoples **plot** in **vain**?*

He stopped and looked at me. "People think this text is about **wrath**," he said. "It isn't. It is about **a mindset**."

The **wicked** in the psalm, he explained, are not monsters. They are the **scoffer** at the next desk, the man who holds everything serious **in derision**. Such people appear **to prosper**, but they are **chaff** — the dry husk that the **wind** carries away.

"And the **fury**?" I asked. "The **kindled** anger, the promise to **dash** them in pieces?"

"That is the part I skip," he said, and smiled. "I am too old for **fury**. I only need the last line."

The last line is about **refuge**.

Something in me **melted** a little. My grandfather had been **a warrior** in a war almost nobody remembers, and here he was, **preparing** for something much quieter, with his **thoughts** in order.

It was **touching** in a way I could not explain to myself on the drive home.
