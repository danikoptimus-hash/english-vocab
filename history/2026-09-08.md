## 1. The Threshing Floor

The psalm opens like a farmer's complaint. *I am **languishing***, the writer says. *Hear my **plea**.*

A bad year had stripped the fields. The **grain** gave out, the **flock** thinned, and the **livestock** died in the pens. Men who had **flattered** him at the gate a year earlier now **walked past** without looking. The book is full of **groans**, and it does not tidy them away.

He does not **pretend to be** innocent. He names his own **transgression** plainly and asks for the **rebuke** he expects.

**However**, the psalm turns. What follows is not judgment but **steadfast** love — the writer says he was handled **tenderly**, the way you handle a **wounded** animal.

He remembers the years of **abundance** before the famine: the threshing floor buried in **chaff**, the **wind** carrying the husks off the hill while the heavy **grain** fell back onto the stone.

Then he turns on his enemies. The **wicked**, he writes, **plot** in **vain**. A **scoffer** may **prosper** for a season, but his **prosperity** is **chaff** too. Their **rage** is nothing; their **fury** will be **kindled** and burn out; God will **dash** them like clay pots and **cast** them **out** of the assembly.

**Nevertheless** — and this is the line the old **reverend** always stopped on — the psalm ends without triumph over anybody. It ends with a **shield**.

*You are my **refuge***, it says. And then, quietly, the writer **exults**: not because his enemies fell, but because his **wages** were not what he had earned.

I have heard my grandmother **chant** these lines over a **sink** of dishes. She kept a wooden **cross** above the door and had no theology at all. When I asked her what the psalm meant, she said it means you can **defile** everything you touch for forty years and still be allowed **to ask for forgiveness**.

The **pillar** of the whole book, she said, is that one idea. Not that people are good — that the door stays open. Her **thoughts** on the subject were shorter than any sermon I have heard since, and she wore the same grey **garment** to church for twenty years without once being ashamed of it.

## 2. Cold Creek, Twenty Years On

## 3. The Mirror Game

Four students played **Truth or Dare** in a **cellar** in November, which was the first mistake.

The dare was simple: **look into the mirror** at midnight, say a name three times, and describe what you see. The **identity** of the woman in the story changes with whoever tells it, and there is a **coincidence** in every version that nobody can explain.

Ilya went first. He described a room behind his own shoulder, **completely different** from the one he was standing in, with a silver **goblet** on a table and a **wire** running along the skirting board.

**After a while** the others stopped laughing. Something in the glass had begun **to come to life** — a shape that **walked past** behind him, **upside down**, and did not stop when he did.

The thing in the house was **vengeful**. ## 4. What the Controller Heard

The **air traffic controller** on duty that night was twenty-six and had done the job for eleven months.

Flight 212 **took off** at 22:40 into clear weather. **Within seconds** of turning north, the crew reported smoke from the **air vents**. The **defect**, a report would say much later, was **caused by** a bracket that had been fitted the wrong way round in a hangar six years earlier.

The captain tried **to level** the aircraft. He said, calmly, that they **had almost no time left**. Then the frequency went quiet.

The **wreckage** came down on a ridge **next to** an old **camp site**, four kilometres from a new **housing development** built **on former Native American land**. Eleven people lived. **For the first time** in her life she did not want to be alone in a room.

## 5. After Another Case

## 6. The Brothers' Verdict

He came **face to face with** Nadia once, in the corridor outside the courtroom, and neither of them said a word. Two of his friends **managed to escape** charges entirely.

## 7. The Road After the Fire

Nadia left the city in July and **hit the road** with no plan at all.

Her sister told the family what Nadia had said **yesterday** — except that in the retelling it became what she had said **the day before**, and by the third telling nobody was sure which day it had been, or whether she had **gone missing** altogether.

She had to **pull over** twice on the mountain road: once because a dog **chased** the car for half a kilometre, and once because she was **overwhelmed** by something she could not name and had to sit with the engine off.

The **townspeople** in the village were kind in the way people are kind to strangers who will leave. They asked whether her brother **might still be alive**. She said she did not know. They **refused** to let her pay for the room.

"**That's really reassuring**," she said, and for once meant it.

There was a **cockroach** in the bathroom and a **pit** behind the house where they burned rubbish, and she slept better there than she had in a year.

Before dawn she went **outside** to sit on the step. A wasp **stung** her wrist while she was tying a boot. **Barely** awake, she watched the light come up over the ridge, and something **awakened** in her that had been asleep since March.

The old house had **burned down** that spring. The **accident scene** report blamed an **electric shock** in the meter cupboard; the family blamed **fate**; Nadia blamed nobody, which took her the longest.

Her father had **performed** the same **prayer** every night of her childhood. She found herself saying it again in a bakery kitchen at five in the morning, badly remembered and out of order.

She had gone south **toward** the coast **in search of** work and **ended up** with flour on her hands. She spent August **searching for** a room she could afford, and September not looking for anything at all.

She met Tomas at the bakery. She **fell in love with** him slowly, over four months, and they announced their **engagement** at Christmas, which her sister called the most **touching** thing she had heard all year.

By then she **had almost no time left** on the visa. She **dragged** the decision out for three weeks and then simply stayed.

The last note in her book that year was about the **offspring** of the goats on the mountain: that they are born in the road, stand up within minutes, and nobody ever tells them the road is dangerous.
